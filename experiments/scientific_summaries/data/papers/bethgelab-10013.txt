Title: A Single-domain Protein Catenane of Dihydrofolate Reductase
Authors: Jing Fang (Peking University); Tianzuo Li (Peking University); Jiyeon Lee (Pohang University of Science and Technology (POSTECH)); Dahye Lim (Pohang University of Science and Technology (POSTECH)); Lianjie Xu (Peking University); Yajie Liu (Peking University); Jongcheol Seo (Pohang University of Science and Technology (POSTECH)); Wen-Bin Zhang (Peking University)

Abstract:
A single-domain protein catenane refers to two mechanically interlocked polypeptide rings that fold synergistically into a compact and integrated structure, which is extremely rare in nature. Herein, we report a single-domain protein catenane of dihydrofolate reductase (cat-DHFR).
The design was achieved by rewiring the connectivity between secondary motifs to introduce artificial entanglement and the synthesis was readily accomplished by a series of programmed streamlined post-translational processing events in cells without any additional in vitro reactions. The target P-2 molecule contains few exogenous motifs and has been thoroughly characterized by combined techniques of LC-MS, SDS-PAGE, protease cleavage experiment, and ion mobility mass spectrometry. Compared to the linear control, cat-DHFR retains the catalytic capability and exhibits enhanced stability against thermal or chemical denaturation due to conformational restriction. The results suggest that linear proteins may be converted into concatenated single-domain counterparts with almost identical chemical composition, well-preserved function, and elevated stability, which represents an entirely new horizon in protein science.


## Introduction
Nature uses protein domains as modules to tailor existing functions and cultivate new functions because they can exist independently, fold autonomously, and evolve dynamically. 1 Natural protein domains are based on linear polypeptide chains whose genes can be conveniently "edited" and "slotted" in the genome during evolution. As such, their backbone has quite limited topological diversity. Though scarce, the discovery of natural topological proteins suggests that proteins with unconventional chemical topology could potentially lead to enhanced protein stability relative to their linear counterparts. [2] [3] [4] In recent years, topology has thus been recognized as a unique dimension for protein engineering. [5] [6] [7] [8] [9] Thanks to the "assembly-reaction" synergy, the design and synthesis of artificial topological proteins have advanced tremendously, 4, [10] [11] [12] which offers access to a variety of topological proteins including star proteins, 13 cyclic proteins, [14] [15] [16] [17] [18] [19] [20] [21] [22] lasso proteins, 23, 24 protein catenanes, [25] [26] [27] [28] [29] [30] [31] and protein pretzelanes. 32 The approach often involves the use of genetically encoded entangling motifs and reaction motifs. As such, the artificial topological proteins synthesized to date usually contain multiple domains, which complicates the study of topological effects on structureproperty relationship. Ideally, a single-domain topological protein shall exhibit the most profound P-3 impact of chemical topology and provide an unusual scaffold for further engineering and evolution.
Yet, it remains challenging to prepare such single-domain topological proteins, even for the simplest [2]catenane, or the Hopf link.
A single protein domain usually has one closely packed hydrophobic core and consists of rigid secondary motifs connected by flexible loops. The highly conserved hydrophobic core allows extensive engineering of the structure, such as circular permutation, [33] [34] [35] [36] domain swapping, [37] [38] [39] loop rethreading, 40 and split-reconstitution. 41, 42 The mutual complementarity among segments preserves the structural integrity despite distinct connectivity. Previously, both green fluorescent protein 43 and dihydrofolate reductase 40 (DHFR) have been shown to tolerate rethreading, giving a linear variant with a swapped sequence that still folds into an overall similar structure with more or less preserved function. Rewiring has also been performed on the SpyTag-SpyCatcher complex, leading to the development of active template for protein heterocatenane synthesis. 29 Recently, we reported the use of mutually orthogonal split-intein ligation pairs to facilitate a streamlined synthesis of protein heterocatenanes with minimal residuals from ligation. 25, 26 We envision that the need for an exogenous entangling motif as the template for catenane synthesis may be further eliminated by exploiting the spatially entangled relationships among the secondary structures and introducing artificial entanglement into the original domain via rewiring the loop region. Using the original fold itself as the entangling template, single-domain protein catenanes may be designed and synthesized, even with identical composition to that of the linear wild-type.
In this contribution, we demonstrate the feasibility of this approach by converting a model enzyme, DHFR from Escherichia coli, into its single-domain catenane counterpart (cat-DHFR).
DHFR catalyzes the reduction of 7,8-dihydrofolate (DHF) to 5,6,7,8-tetrahydrofolate (THF) in the P-4 presence of cofactor nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate (NADPH). Despite numerous works on its engineering and evolution, none goes beyond the linear backbone as defined by the template polymerization mechanism of the cellular ribosomal machinery. We show that it is possible to change the chemical topology of a protein domain without collapsing the fold and to bring in profound changes in properties.

There are even more designs when taking the variation in the length and sequence of the new loops into consideration. A successful design should not only ensure the entangled conformation but also embrace high reconstitution efficiency. For the former, the extent of entanglement may be assessed by the Gaussian linking number (GLN) matrix 44 of the wt-DHFR (PDB: 4KJJ) to identify the splitting position (Figure 1d ). The split of DHFR that gives the largest GLN is believed to possess the potential entangling core and new loops were then designed with distinct lengths to ensure proper crossing.

For enzymes, it is hoped that the activity will not be affected. Hence, the active site and the adjacent regions should be left intact if possible. Combining these criteria, we identified that cleavage at the position between the α-helix-C and β-sheet-5 (residues 88 and 89) would generate two highly intertwined segments (GLN +0.726, Figure S1a ), which could be converted to catenane topology upon cyclization of the two fragments. Thus, a design composed of residues 1-88 as one ring and residues 89-159 as the other ring was adopted (Figure 1a ). The two fragments are known to reconstitute efficiently. 42, 45 The entangling core of the design is similar to that of the wild-type and the linker naturally extends to form the ring, giving a cat-DHFR with a GLN of +1.120 (Figure S1b ).
We then proceeded to design a synthetic strategy for this molecule. Cellular Synthesis of cat-DHFR. The streamlined biosynthesis using two pairs of mutually orthogonal split-intein pairs 25, 26 provides a straightforward way to synthesize cat-DHFR using split DHFR itself as the entangled template. To ensure the reconstitution and cyclization of the two polypeptide chains while keeping the correct spatial relationship, the length of the new loop and the sites of ring closure should be carefully chosen, as the retrosynthetic analysis shown in Figure 1a .
Considering that cyclization efficiency depends on the distance between the new termini and that the two bulky split-intein pairs may interfere with each other, ring I was circularly permutated to generate new termini between residues 23 and 24, and the rewired loops were designed to be long enough ( 16residues) to bridge the split structural motifs. The gene cassette was then designed containing two DHFR segments as the entangling template, two mutually orthogonal split-intein pairs for ring closure, and the tobacco etch virus (TEV) protease recognition sequence in the first ring for subsequent proof of topology by proteolytic digestion experiment (Figure 1b ). A linear DHFR with identical amino acid composition (l-DHFR) was then designed as the linear control bearing extra amino acids not present in the wild-type at the N-and C-terminus (Figure 1a and 1c , See SI for sequence information of the two constructs).

## P-7
The genes of cat-DHFR and l-DHFR were synthesized and sub-cloned to pMCSG19 and pET21a vectors, respectively, for expression. The plasmids harboring these genes were used to transform E.coli BL21(DE3) competent cells. Protein expression was carried out at 16 o C for 20 h to ensure proper folding and complete reaction. After cell lysis, proteins were purified under native conditions using Ni-NTA affinity chromatography. The eluted products were further purified by size exclusion chromatography (SEC). SDS-PAGE analysis of purified products shows a single band at a molecular weight of approximately 22 kDa for l-DHFR and a major band at less than 20 kDa for cat-DHFR (Figure 2a ). There are some faint bands at lower molecular weights, which are likely attributed to the individual component rings. The apparently faster mobility of cat-DHFR in SDS-PAGE is reasonable because the concatenated form tends to be much more compact than fully unfolded linear chains. The SEC overlay shows that cat-DHFR elutes as a much broader peak at a slightly larger retention volume than l-DHFR (Figure 2b ). It is consistent with SDS-PAGE that cat-DHFR has a more compact structure and further suggests that cat-DHFR may have a quite dynamic structure due to the coupled movement of two mechanically interlocked rings. The identities of cat-DHFR and l-DHFR were confirmed by their accurate molecular weights in LC-MS spectra as expected (Figure 2c ). The far-UV CD spectra further show that the cat-DHFR remains folded, yet has substantially reduced intensities of both positive peak in ellipticity at ~200 nm and negative peak in ellipticity between 210 nm and 230 nm, as compared to the linear control, suggesting a loosening of the secondary structure (Figure 2d ). According to the literature, [45] [46] [47] similar change has been observed in circularly permutated linear DHFR. It seems that circular permutation may cause minor local disturbance on the local folded structures, but the overall structure is intact. 46 The near-UV CD spectrum of cat-DHFR exhibits a positive ellipticity at ~290 nm similar to the linear control (Figure S2 ). Although we have not been able to obtain a crystal structure of cat-DHFR (vide infra), the existence of a folded structure in cat-DHFR, though slightly deviated from the linear one, is without question, which is also consistent with the role of folding in catenane formation. It is a prerequisite as a template for catenane formation, while it is also reinforced by the catenane topology.

## P-9
It is worth mentioning that the current approach requires extensive optimization to achieve the "assembly-reaction" synergy for high-yield expression and efficient catenane formation. We also attempted the construct using split-intein ligation pairs on the same side of DHFR for ring closure with similar amino acid composition and linker length (Figure S3a ). However, much less catenane product was formed with considerable amounts of single-ring side products (~33% of catenane form).
It suggested that the steric hindrance of the bulky split-intein pairs affects the correct reconstitution efficiency and cyclization. In this case, circular permutation is necessary in order to place the cyclization motifs on the opposite side of DHFR. Even so, there are many different sites for ring closure, preferably at the loop regions (Figure 1a , see Supporting Information for sequences). Among these possibilities, the site between residues 23 and 24 was found to be the most efficient one (Figure S3b-d ). The length and sequence of the newly added loop may also play an important role. In this work, we mainly choose innocent flexible linkers with embedded elements essential for purification or proof of topology. To probe the linker effect, we tested L2 of ring II with different sequences and lengths (Figure S4 ). As the L2 becomes shorter, the split-intein cannot efficiently reconstitute and react, therefore the majority of precursors form monocyclic by-products, leading to a decrease in the yield of catenane. However, we noticed that although the yield decreased to a certain extent and the proportion of monocyclic by-products and oligomers increased slightly, the catenated products could still be obtained (Figure S4 ). Notably, the yield with the shortest linker is only about one-fifth that of the longest (~12 mg/L of culture). Hence, the method is generally robust before the linker becomes too short.
Proof of Topology for cat-DHFR. An unambiguous proof of topology would come from crystallography. We have attempted to crystallize cat-DHFR, but failed to obtain crystals of sufficient P-10
quality. It may be due to unsatisfactory purity, the existence of disordered linkers, or reduced binding affinity with substrates, etc. We thus had to resort to indirect ways of characterization, such as proteolytic digestion experiment similar to previous reports. 15, 25, 26, 28, 29, 32, 48 A tobacco etch virus (TEV) protease recognition site was pre-programmed in the first ring of cat-DHFR. Upon cleavage, cat-DHFR was converted to a linear fragment and a cyclic fragment as expected, which was confirmed by the SDS-PAGE analysis and LC-MS spectra (Figure 2e ). Further evidence was obtained by advanced mass spectrometry (MS)-related techniques including ion mobility spectrometry (IMS)
and collision-induced dissociation (CID). It has been reported that different topologies often produce distinguishable differences either in collision cross-section (CCS) values in IMS or in the secondary product ions generated from CID. 23, 32, 49 Thus, both IMS-MS and CID were applied to characterize l-DHFR and cat-DHFR.

## P-13
Activity is one of the most critical parameters for enzymes. The catalytic activity of DHFR can be readily assayed by dihydrofolate reduction with NADPH. The kinetic curves of DHFR samples were obtained by monitoring the absorbance at 340 nm at 25 o C (Figure 4c ). Considering the extensive engineering performed on the structure, it was not surprising at all to see that cat-DHFR has a lower activity (~5 U/mg) than the linear control (~14 U/mg). Notably, the previously reported rethreaded DHFR shows no reducing activity at all. 40 In fact, even circular permutation alone could cause the decrease of catalytic activity to varying degrees. 45, 47 The reconstituted physical complex of two DHFR fragments (split between α-helix-C and β-sheet-5) retained only 15% of the enzymatic activity of the full-length DHFR. 45 Therefore, despite extensive engineering and variation in sequence and topology, cat-DHFR remarkably retains a significant portion of the activity. We envision that the reduced activity may be further restored to match, if not surpass, that the linear control by directed evolution.
We measured the kinetic parameters Km and kcat by fitting the Michaelis curve and Hanes-Woolf plot (Figure S7 and Table 1 ). The cat-DHFR has a Km approximately 5-fold that of l-DHFR and a kcat about 38% that of l-DHFR, consistent with the reduced activity upon concatenation. And the catalytic efficiency (kcat/ Km) of cat-DHFR is reduced by an order of magnitude. The results suggest that cat-DHFR has a lower affinity toward substrates, a lower turnover rate, and a decreased catalytic efficiency. To verify the change in affinity, we determined their binding affinity with NADPH, DHF, and THF using isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC). As shown in Table 1 , the disassociation constants (KD) are generally much larger for cat-DHFR than that for l-DHFR with the corresponding substrates (~173-fold for DHF and ~14-fold for NADPH). Considering that the Km of cat-DHFR is only 5-fold that of l-DHFR at a saturation concentration of NADPH, we assume that the binding of P-14
NADPH significantly enhances the affinity of cat-DHFR towards DHF. Interestingly, the KD between cat-DHFR and the product, THF, is also increased by ~3.6-fold relative to l-DHFR, which means that the product can also easily leave the enzyme. Therefore, the reduced enzymatic activity should be mainly attributed to the compromised affinity of cat-DHFR towards substrate and cofactor. o C. We also tentatively tested the samples' tolerance to organic solvents by adding DMSO to a final concentration of 5% and performing the activity assay. It seems that cat-DHFR has preserved more activity (87%) than l-DHFR (74%) (Figure S11 ). Both results suggest that mechanical interlocking enhances the thermal resilience of the enzyme.
