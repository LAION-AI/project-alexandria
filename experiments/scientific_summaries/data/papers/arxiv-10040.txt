Frustration-driven successive metamagnetic transitions in TbB4
T. Inami,1, ∗K. Ohwada,1 Y. H. Matsuda,2 Z. W. Ouyang,2
H. Nojiri,2 D. Okuyama,3 T. Matsumura,3 and Y. Murakami3
1Synchrotron Radiation Research Unit, Japan Atomic Energy Agency, Sayo, Hyogo 679-5148, Japan
2 Institute for Materials Research, Tohoku University, 2-1-1 Katahira, Sendai 980-8577, Japan
3 Department of Physics, Faculty of Science, Tohoku University, Aoba, Sendai 980-8578, Japan
(Dated: February 6, 2014)
Resonant magnetic x-ray diﬀraction experiments on the Shastry-Sutherland lattice TbB4 were
carried out under strong pulsed magnetic ﬁelds up to 29 T. TbB4 exhibits a multi-step magnetization
process above 16 T when magnetic ﬁelds are applied along the c-axis. This magnetization curve is,
however, hardly predicted, because TbB4 has an XY -type ground state. We observed the intensity
of the 100 magnetic reﬂection as a function of magnetic ﬁeld and found that the magnetization
plateau phases are accompanied by large XY components of magnetic moments. A simple spin
model is deduced from the data and the magnetization is calculated using this model. Finally we
propose that frustration is the key to understanding the observed multi-step magnetization.

When one applies pressure to a substance, the volume
will be reduced at all times depending on its compress-
ibility. In contrast to such natural behavior, several mag-
netic materials exhibit magnetization plateaus, where the
magnetization keeps a constant value at a ﬁnite range
of magnetic ﬁelds even below the saturation ﬁeld. This
“zero compressibility” is simply ascribed to the fact that
Sz is a good quantum number, while a variety of mech-
anisms give rise to plateaus in the magnetization curve.
The easy-axis magnetization of an Ising antiferromag-
net is most comprehensible.
An example is the one-
third magnetization plateau of CoCl2·2H2O shown in
Fig. 1(a) [1]; a combination of a ﬁnite number of Ising
spins produces step-like magnetization. The multi-step
magnetization curves of PrCo2Si2 [2] and CeSb [3] are
also classiﬁed into this category. The plateau phases are
described by a complex seaquence of up- and down-spins.
Magnetization plateaus are observed not only for Ising
spins but also for Heisenberg spins. Isolated (or weakly
interacting) dimers are a trivial case. Cs3Cr2Cl9 (S= 3
2
dimers) and Ba3Mn2O8 (S=1 dimers) exhibit three-step
and two-step magnetization curves, respectively [4, 5]. In
the orthogonally connected S= 1
2 dimers SrCu2(BO3)2,
superstructures of excited triplet dimers are stabilized
and one-eighth, one-fourth, and one-third magnetization
plateaus are observed [6, 7].
Geometrically frustrated Heisenberg antiferromagnets
also frequently exhibit a magnetization plateau.

No ordered magnetic moment perpendicular to the mag-

Recently, a multi-step magnetization curve was ob-
served in TbB4. Intriguingly, several experimental evi-
dences indicate that TbB4 resembles none of the above-
mentioned magnets. In the rest of this letter, we show
that TbB4 indeed belongs to another rare class of mag-
nets, characterized by ordered perpendicular components
in the plateau phase. To our best knowledge, CsFeCl3 is
considered to be the only example [12]. In these mag-
nets, frustration plays a central role in their multi-step
magnetization process.
The rare-earth tetra-boride RB4 crystallizes into a
tetragonal structure (space group: P4/mbm). The net-
work of the rare-earth ions is equivalent to the frustrated
Shastry-Sutherland (SS) lattice [13].
Several pieces of
work thus have been carried out from a viewpoint of frus-
tration between rare-earth quadrupole moments [14, 15].
Somewhat confusingly, in the strict sense, classical con-
tinuous spins on the SS lattice are not frustrated, because
the ground state is a unique helical or N´eel structure and

2
has no degeneracy [13]. However, for helical stuructures,
each antiferromagnetic bond is not antiparallel and thus
local pair exchange energy is not minimized. Each spin is
not satisﬁed and still feels “frustration”. We will revisit
this point later.
In TbB4, there are two transition points TN1=44 K
and TN1=22 K. The total angular momentum of the Tb
ion is large (J=6) and hence the Tb spin is approximated
by a classical spin. The magnetic structure between TN1
and TN2 revealed by neutron powder diﬀraction experi-
ments is shown in Fig. 2(a) [16]. The magnetic moments
are conﬁned in the basal ab-plane. Additional in-plane
anisotropy orients the moments parallel to the diagonal
line. The nearest-neighbor interaction J1 is antiferromag-
netic. The next-nearest-neighbor interaction J2 is prob-
ably antiferromagnetic, and J1 and J2 form a frustrated
SS lattice. In RB4, the further-neighbor antiferromag-
netic interaction J3 is taken into account and stabilizes
the observed magnetic structure. In this structure, the J2
bond is not satisﬁed. A Dzialoshinsky-Moriya type inter-
action is also expected because only the positive chirality
state is selected. Below TN2, the magnetic moments ro-
tate in the ab-plane and a collinear-like antiferromagnetic
structure is realized. This transition is accompanied by
a structural change, probably in order to gain antiferro-
magnetic exchange energy at the cost of elastic energy.
When a magnetic ﬁeld is applied to such XY -type
magnets along the z(c)-axis, one may anticipate a linear
magnetization process, because the magnetic moments
simply slant toward the magnetic ﬁeld. On the contrary,
the magnetization process of TbB4 parallel to the c-axis
exhibits multi-step metamagnetic behavior between 16
and 28 T as shown in Fig. 2(b) [17]. The number of the
steps is reported to be 9, and the one-half magnetization
plateau is most prominent. Although the metamagnetic
behavior suggests that there are low-lying Ising excited
states just above the XY ground state, the origin of the
multiple successive transitions is not easily understood.
Diﬀraction experiments are the most powerful tool
for investigating an ordered regular array of atoms and
magnetic moments.
Recently, x-ray diﬀraction experi-
ments under strong pulsed magnetic ﬁelds have been in-
tensively developed at several places utilizing brilliant
third-generation synchrotron x-rays [18, 19, 20].
Now
a structural phase transition above 30 T can be readily
observed using these state-of-the-art technologies. How
about magnetic phase transitions? X-rays do have mag-
netic scattering cross-sections.
Although these values
are usually very small, when one tunes the x-ray energy
through an absorption edge of an element, the magnetic
scattering cross section of the element becomes large. In
the hard x-ray regime, where conventional x-ray diﬀrac-
tion experiments can be performed, this eﬀect, known
as resonance enhancement, is particularly large for rare-
earth elements [21].

0.6 ms. The magnet is a split-pair, and two windows are
prepared for incoming and outgoing x-rays. The magnet
and a sample were attached to the cold head of a conven-
tional closed cycle refrigerator. Single crystals of TbB4
were grown by the ﬂoating zone method.

tensity as a function of time with a typical resolution
of 10 µs.
The ﬁeld strength was also monitored as a
function of time, and we obtained the ﬁeld dependence
of the diﬀraction intensity from these two series of data.
The sample surface was locally heated by irradiation of
synchrotron x-rays. We estimated the true sample tem-
perature by measuring the temperature dependence of
the magnetic peak intensity at each irradiation intensity.

the one-half magnetization plateau is observed.
From
this result, we understand that the plateau phases include
the (1,0,0) component in their magnetic structures.
The second important outcome is obtained from the
characteristic polarization dependence of RMXD. The
scattering amplitude is proportional to m·(ǫi×ǫf), where
m is the magnetic moment and ǫi,f are the polariza-
tion vectors of the incident and scattered x-rays, respec-
tively [22]. The incident polarization is only horizontal
(π), and the scattering plane is also horizontal. Scattered
x-rays of horizontal polarization (π′) therefore observe
the vertical component of the magnetic moments, which
is the c-axis component in this experiment, whereas the
scattered x-rays of vertical polarization (σ′) sense mag-
netic moments parallel to the incident x-rays, which are
the magnetic moments in the ab-plane. It should also
be noted that the π-π′ channel is proportional to sin 2θ,
where θ is the Bragg angle. Since θ for the 100 reﬂection
is only 6.6◦, the scattering amplitude for the c-axis com-
ponent is very small. Hence it is found that the observed
large (1,0,0) component in the plateau phases consists of
the ab-plane components.
This is a rather surprising result, because it is expected
that all the magnetic moments in plateau phases are par-
allel or antiparallel to the magnetic ﬁeld as mentioned in
the introduction. A canted magnetic structure, usually
derived from the coexistence of parallel and perpendic-
ular components, results in a continuous magnetization
curve unlike the observed step-like magnetization pro-
cess. Accordingly, we submit an idea that in TbB4 the
magnetic structures in the plateau phases consist of very
hard XY -type magnetic moments and Ising-like mag-
netic moments.

Many states are degenerate at Hc1/2 when E=∞. When E
is ﬁnite, the one-third plateau phase, for instance, reduces its
energy, while the one-half plateau phase does not.
Hence, when a defect is introduced, the magnetic struc-
ture around the defect relaxes and gains exchange energy.
This does not happen in magnetic lattices without frus-
tration, because all exchange energies have already been
minimized.
In the one-third plateau phase, the magnetic moments
are rearranged and reduce the total energy as shown in
the ﬁgure.
In contrast, the resultant magnetic lattice
of the one-half plateau phase is not frustrated, as il-
lustrated by collinear in-plane components. Hence the
critical ﬁeld of the one-third plateau phase Hc1/3 is in-
evitably smaller than the critical ﬁeld of the one-half
plateau phase Hc1/2 (see Fig. 4(c)). For magnetic sys-
tems with short-range interactions, it is expected that
small commensurate unit cells are more stable than large
or incommensurate cells. Thus it is plausible that a ﬁ-
nite number of selected magnetization steps appear in
the magnetization curve through this mechanism. Inci-
dentally, interactions further than J3 must be included
to account for the splitting at Hc1.
The proposed spin model (very hard XY ground state,
excited Ising spins and no cross-term between them) is
very much simpliﬁed.
However, we believe that this
model captures basic features of the real TbB4.
We
would also like to emphasize that the spin models for
TbB4 and CsFeCl3 are quite diﬀerent. Nevertheless, the
basic scenario that frustration lifts the degeneracy at a
metamagnetic transition is common for both magnets.
We can state that frustration-driven successive metam-
agnetic transitions are a general mechanism.
In conclusion, we have investigated the multi-step mag-
netization process of TbB4 using RMXD. The magnetic
intensity observed in the plateau phases unambiguously
illustrates that large XY -components of the magnetic
moments coexist with aligned Z-components.
A sim-
ple spin model reproduces the one-half magnetization
plateau, and it is found that the frustrated nature of the
SS lattice lifts the degeneracy at the critical ﬁelds and
creates the observed multi-step metamagnetic behavior.
Paradoxically, frustration is not only an origin of degen-
erate states, but also breaks degeneracy.
Finally, we stress that integration of two advanced
experimental techniques, RMXD using brilliant third-
generation synchrotron radiation and excellent pulsed-
magnetic-ﬁeld techniques, enables us to explore magnetic
structures at high magnetic ﬁelds close to 30 T. Although
this is still a challenging experiment, the technique will be
widely applied in the near future to the high-ﬁeld phases
of many interesting magnets.
