Executive Summary
This paper introduces BATI, a novel Bayesian rare variant association test using Integrated Nested Laplace Approximation (INLA) within the 'Rare Variant Genome Wide Association Study' (rvGWAS) framework. BATI efficiently integrates variant-specific features as covariates, outperforming existing rare variant association methods, especially when incorporating biological context like functional annotation. The study addresses the challenge of low statistical power in rare variant association studies (RVAS), particularly in small to medium-sized cohorts or when genetic variation explains a small fraction of phenotypic variance. BATI employs a Bayesian approach using INLA for full model estimation, enabling the inclusion of variant characteristics as covariates. The rvGWAS framework combines quality control, filtering, detection of data stratification, functional variant annotation, and several RVAS tests, including BATI. Results from semi-synthetic whole-exome sequencing cohorts demonstrate BATI's superior performance, achieving power above 75% in scenarios where competing tests fail. The study highlights the importance of incorporating biological context and shows that BATI is robust to complex population structure. The primary limitation is the reliance on semi-synthetic cohorts, which, while realistic, may not fully capture the complexity of real-world datasets. The study's significance lies in providing a more powerful and flexible method for identifying genetic associations with complex diseases, particularly when the genetic contribution to the phenotype is small.

Research Context
Rare variant association studies (RVAS) aim to identify disease loci based on the burden of rare and damaging variants within genomic units. Traditional methods like GWAS lack power for RVAS due to the low frequency of rare variants. Existing RVAS methods, such as burden tests and variance component tests (SKAT, C-alpha), have limitations. Burden tests are powerful when a high proportion of rare variants in a gene affect its function, but this is often not the case. Variance component tests consider heterogeneous effects among rare variants, but they may still lack power. More recent methods, like HBMR and MiST, have been developed to address these limitations. MiST incorporates variant characteristics but requires a score test, which limits its ability to estimate the full model. This work addresses the need for a more powerful and flexible RVAS method that can efficiently integrate variant characteristics and perform full model estimation, which is what BATI provides.

Research Question Hypothesis
The central research question is whether a Bayesian rare variant association test using Integrated Nested Laplace Approximation (BATI) can outperform existing RVAS methods by efficiently integrating variant characteristics. The primary hypothesis is that BATI will achieve higher statistical power compared to established methods, particularly when incorporating biological context, such as functional annotation. The study predicts that BATI will be more effective in identifying risk genes, even when the genetic variation explains a small fraction of phenotypic variance. The alternative hypothesis is that BATI will not significantly improve performance compared to existing methods, or that its performance will be comparable. The study uses semi-synthetic whole-exome sequencing cohorts to test these hypotheses. Success is defined by BATI's ability to identify risk genes with higher power and accuracy than existing methods, especially in scenarios where other tests fail. The study differs from prior work by using INLA for full model estimation, allowing for the flexible integration of variant characteristics as covariates, and by implementing the method within the rvGWAS framework.

Methodological Details
The study employed a Bayesian rare variant Association Test using Integrated Nested Laplace Approximation (BATI) within the rvGWAS framework for rare variant association analysis. The study design involved the creation of semi-synthetic whole-exome sequencing (WES) cohorts to benchmark BATI against existing RVAS methods. The study used two large WES datasets: the 1000 Genomes Project (1000GP) and an Iberian cohort. Samples were randomly assigned to case or control groups. Risk variants for breast cancer were introduced into the VCF files of cases. The ClinVar database was queried for breast cancer risk variants annotated as exonic or splicing. Variants with a minor allele frequency (MAF) higher than 0.01 in any ancestry population in EVS, 1000GP, or ExAC were removed. The study used the R-INLA project (R package INLA version 17.06.20) to build a hierarchical Bayesian approach to the GLMM for the association of rare variants with phenotypes in the context of case-control studies. The phenotype of the *i*-th individual, *Y*i, was assumed to belong to an exponential family. Model selection was performed using the Deviance Information Criterion (DIC). The difference in DIC (ΔDIC) was used to compare models. To estimate the probability of type I error, individuals were randomly assigned to cases or controls. Models under null and alternative hypotheses were adjusted for each gene or biological unit. Empirical distributions of ΔDIC were obtained. A ΔDIC threshold was selected from the quantile corresponding to the desired significance level. For more robust threshold estimation, S datasets were generated by randomly shuffling cases and controls, and the median of the thresholds was used. S was set to 10. The study used Burden, KBAC, SKAT-O, MiST, HBMR, and BATI for comparison. The study used 100 simulations of risk variants for each gene separately.

Procedures Architectures
BATI, the core method, is a Bayesian rare variant association test implemented using Integrated Nested Laplace Approximation (INLA). INLA is a numerical approximation procedure designed to estimate hierarchical latent Gaussian Markov random field models. The model is a hierarchical Bayesian approach to the generalized linear mixed model (GLMM) for the association of rare variants with phenotypes in case-control studies. The model efficiently integrates categorical and numerical characteristics of genetic variants as covariates. The rvGWAS framework, in which BATI is implemented, combines quality control, interactive filtering, detection of data stratification, integration of functional variant annotations, and several RVAS tests (Burden, SKAT-O, KBAC, MiST, HBMR, and BATI). The study used semi-synthetic cohorts generated by combining real WES datasets with known risk variants for breast cancer. The simulator randomly assigns WES samples to cases or controls and introduces predisposition variants found in ClinVar into the VCF files of cases. The model specification includes a linear predictor for the observed phenotype, incorporating variant characteristics as covariates. Model selection is based on the Deviance Information Criterion (DIC). The study used the R-INLA project (R package INLA version 17.06.20). The study used real WES cohorts, representing realistic background noise, with real disease variants, featuring realistic functional impact profiles and variant distributions, to form semi-synthetic benchmark cohorts.

Key Results
BATI outperformed existing RVAS methods in semi-synthetic WES cohorts. BATI achieved power above 75% in scenarios where competing tests failed to identify risk genes. For a low variance explained (VE) of 0.5% and a strict TIER of 0.01%, all methods except BATI showed power close to 0, while BATI achieved power close to 100% for three risk genes (median power of 55%). At 0.01% TIER, only 2 genes (out of ~20,000) are expected as significant by chance. BATI showed relatively small differences in performance across different genes, while Burden, KBAC, and SKAT-O showed considerable variation. For a VE of 0.5%, Burden, KBAC, and SKAT-O recurrently failed to call BRCA2, and HBMR failed to call BARD1, CHEK2, and PALB2. In the gene-wise power analysis, BATI called most genes with TIER 0.01% even at VE of 0.5%, and all genes at TIER 0.1% with >80% power (Table 2). The performance of Burden, KBAC, and SKAT-O varied considerably between genes, while MiST, HBMR, and BATI showed relatively small differences. The performance patterns of Burden, KBAC, and SKAT-O across the 8 risk gene architectures were highly similar. MiST failed to identify any risk gene at low VE or low TIER thresholds.

Interpretation Implications
The findings suggest that BATI is a more powerful and flexible method for identifying genetic associations with complex diseases, particularly when the genetic contribution to the phenotype is small. The superior performance of BATI, especially when incorporating biological context, highlights the importance of integrating variant characteristics in RVAS. The results imply that the choice of RVAS method significantly impacts the ability to detect risk genes, especially at low levels of variance explained. The study supports the hypothesis that methods leveraging variant characteristics and using fast and accurate model estimation can outperform existing methods. The results also suggest that combining different approaches, like Burden- and SKAT-type methods with BATI, may be beneficial to compensate for the weaknesses of individual methods. The study provides a framework for future research by demonstrating the utility of INLA in RVAS and the importance of using realistic benchmarks.

Contradictions Limitations
The study's primary limitation is the use of semi-synthetic cohorts, which, while realistic, may not fully capture the complexity of real-world datasets. The study does not address the potential for population stratification or other confounding factors that may be present in real-world data. The study does not provide information on the computational cost of BATI compared to other methods. The study does not report the specific variant characteristics used as covariates. The study does not provide details on the specific functional annotation databases used. The study does not report the random seed used for the simulations. The study does not report the exact number of individuals in the 1000GP and Iberian cohorts. MiST performed poorly on the realistic WES cohorts, likely due to an issue with zero-inflated p-values caused by inappropriate handling of low variant counts.

Three Takeaways
(1) The core novel contribution is the development of BATI, a Bayesian rare variant association test using INLA, which efficiently integrates variant characteristics and outperforms existing RVAS methods, especially when incorporating biological context. (2) The strongest evidence comes from the gene-wise power analysis, where BATI achieves high power (e.g., close to 100% for three risk genes) at low VE levels (0.5%) and strict TIER thresholds (0.01%), outperforming Burden, KBAC, and SKAT-O. (3) The primary limitation is the reliance on semi-synthetic cohorts, which may not fully reflect the complexities of real-world datasets, and the lack of detail on the specific variant characteristics and functional annotation databases used. A forward path involves validating BATI on diverse real-world datasets and exploring the integration of additional variant characteristics to further enhance its performance.
