Executive Summary
This paper investigates the Cauchy problem for a class of two-dimensional, nonlinear, nonlocal wave equations that model anti-plane shear motions in nonlocal elasticity. The nonlocality is represented by a convolution integral. The study establishes conditions for the global existence and finite-time blow-up of solutions, depending on the initial data's smoothness and the properties of the convolution kernel. The core approach extends techniques used for one-dimensional nonlocal Boussinesq-type wave equations. The main findings include local well-posedness results under specific conditions on the Sobolev space and the kernel's regularity. Global existence is proven under a positivity condition on the nonlinear function and sufficient smoothness of the initial data. A finite-time blow-up result is also established. The novelty lies in extending existing techniques to a two-dimensional wave equation within the context of nonlocal elasticity. This work is significant because it contributes to the understanding of wave propagation in materials where long-range forces are important, particularly at small scales. A key limitation is the reliance on specific conditions for global existence and blow-up, which may not always be met in real-world scenarios.

Research Context
Conventional elasticity theory lacks an intrinsic length scale, failing to account for long-range forces significant at small scales. This limitation hinders the accurate prediction of phenomena like dispersive wave behavior in crystal lattices and stress field boundedness near crack tips. Nonlocal elasticity theory addresses these shortcomings by incorporating long-range interactions, where stress at a point depends on the strain field at every point in the body. Prior research, such as in [1, 2, 3], has explored one-dimensional nonlinear models of nonlocal elasticity. This paper extends these techniques to a two-dimensional wave equation, contributing to the understanding of anti-plane shear motions in nonlocally elastic media. This work builds upon and extends previous research by applying established techniques to a more complex, two-dimensional setting.
