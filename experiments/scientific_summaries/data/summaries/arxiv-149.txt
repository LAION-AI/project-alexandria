Executive Summary
This paper introduces and explores generalized entropy measures, extending Shannon entropy, and their applications in modeling probability distributions and physical phenomena. The authors examine the product probability property (PPP), or statistical independence, and argue that the logarithmic form of Shannon entropy arises from a recursivity postulate rather than the PPP itself. They introduce Mathai’s generalized entropy and demonstrate its connection to pathway models, which describe exponential and power-law behaviors, and to Kerridge’s measure of inaccuracy. The study shows that the maximization of Mathai's entropy leads to various probability models depending on the constraints applied. The core finding is that Mathai's entropy provides a flexible framework for deriving probability distributions, including Tsallis statistics and generalized beta forms, through the maximum entropy principle. The paper highlights the non-additivity of generalized entropies, even under PPP, and derives differential equations that characterize the pathway models. A key limitation is the theoretical focus, with limited empirical validation of the proposed models. The work is significant because it provides a unified approach to derive a variety of probability distributions, offering a broader perspective on entropy and its applications in diverse fields.

Research Context
Shannon entropy, characterized by additivity, is a fundamental concept in information theory. However, it may not be suitable in contexts where additivity is not observed, such as in statistical mechanics. Prior work by Mathai and Rathie (1975, 1976) generalized Shannon entropy, exploring its properties and characterization theorems. Tsallis entropy, Havrda-Charvát entropy, and Rényi entropy are other generalizations of Shannon entropy. These generalized entropies provide alternative measures of uncertainty and information, particularly in non-extensive systems. This paper builds on these prior works, introducing Mathai's generalized entropy and connecting it to pathway models and Kerridge's measure of inaccuracy. The paper addresses the limitations of Shannon entropy in scenarios where additivity does not hold, offering a framework for modeling non-additive systems.
