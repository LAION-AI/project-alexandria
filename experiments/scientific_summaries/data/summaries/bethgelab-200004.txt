Executive Summary
This study investigates the role of sarco/endoplasmic reticulum calcium-ATPase (SERCA) in the replication of paramyxoviruses, specifically Peste des petits ruminants virus (PPRV) and Newcastle disease virus (NDV). The research demonstrates that SERCA facilitates paramyxovirus replication in Vero cells. Treatment with the SERCA-specific inhibitor Thapsigargin significantly reduced virus replication, while overexpression of SERCA rescued this inhibitory effect. The study found that PPRV and NDV infection induced SERCA expression, which could be blocked by Thapsigargin. Time-of-addition and virus step-specific assays indicated that Thapsigargin primarily inhibits viral entry and impacts the subcellular localization of viral proteins. Furthermore, NDV, but not PPRV, acquired a degree of resistance to Thapsigargin upon long-term passage in Vero cells. Key findings include: Thapsigargin at 0.5 µM significantly inhibited NDV and PPRV replication (Figure 1c, 1d); overexpression of SERCA2 rescued the inhibitory effect of Thapsigargin (Figure 2a, 2b); and NDV, but not PPRV, developed resistance to Thapsigargin after 70 passages (Figure 7b, 7c). The novelty lies in identifying SERCA as a host factor supporting paramyxovirus replication and demonstrating that viruses can develop resistance to inhibitors targeting host factors. This research is significant for understanding paramyxovirus replication and identifying SERCA as a potential target for antiviral drug development. A limitation is the lack of a complete understanding of the mechanism of NDV resistance to Thapsigargin.

Research Context
Current antiviral strategies often target viral proteins, which can lead to drug resistance due to high mutation rates. Host factors, which viruses rely on for replication, offer alternative targets. Prior research has identified host factors involved in viral replication, such as SPCA1 in Flaviviridae, Togaviridae, and Paramyxoviridae replication. This study addresses the need for novel antiviral strategies by investigating the role of SERCA, a calcium transporter, in paramyxovirus replication. The study builds on previous work screening kinase and phosphatase inhibitors for antiviral potential against influenza A viruses. This work differs from prior studies by focusing on SERCA's role in paramyxovirus replication and exploring the potential for resistance to host-targeting inhibitors.

Research Question Hypothesis
The central research question is whether SERCA, a host factor, plays a role in paramyxovirus replication and whether it can be targeted for antiviral therapy. The study hypothesizes that inhibiting SERCA will impair paramyxovirus replication. This hypothesis is supported by the prediction that treatment with a SERCA-specific inhibitor, Thapsigargin, will reduce viral titers. The study also predicts that overexpression of SERCA will rescue the inhibitory effect of Thapsigargin, further supporting SERCA's role in replication. The study further investigates whether paramyxoviruses can develop resistance to Thapsigargin, a host-targeting agent. The alternative hypothesis is that SERCA inhibition will not affect viral replication or that viruses will readily develop resistance. This research differs from previous studies by focusing on SERCA as a target and investigating the potential for resistance to an inhibitor targeting a host factor.

Methodological Details
The study employed in vitro experiments using Vero cells (African green monkey kidney), 293T cells (human embryonic kidney), HeLa cells, and goat kidney cells. PPRV and NDV were used as the paramyxoviruses. Thapsigargin (Sigma) was used as a SERCA-specific inhibitor at a non-toxic concentration of 0.5 µM. Cytotoxicity was assessed using an MTT assay. Antiviral efficacy was determined by infecting cells with PPRV/NDV at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 0.1 in the presence of 0.5 µM Thapsigargin or a vehicle control (DMSO), followed by quantification of progeny virus particles using a plaque assay. Virucidal activity was assessed by incubating virus suspensions with Thapsigargin for 1.5 h at 37°C before plaque assaying. Virus step-specific assays (attachment, entry, RNA synthesis, and budding) were performed as previously described. SERCA2 overexpression was achieved by transfecting HeLa/goat kidney cells with a plasmid expressing SERCA2 (pCR3-SERCA2) or an empty vector (pCR3). Immunofluorescence assays were performed to visualize the subcellular localization of viral proteins. Thapsigargin-resistant viral mutants were selected by sequentially passaging PPRV and NDV in Vero cells in the presence of 0.25 µM Thapsigargin or 0.05% DMSO (vehicle control) for 70 passages. Viral titers were determined by plaque assay. The study did not specify a preregistration or multiple-comparison corrections. Ethical approval was not mentioned.

Procedures Architectures
The study utilized Vero cells, 293T cells, HeLa cells, and goat kidney cells, cultured in DMEM supplemented with antibiotics and 10% FBS. PPRV and NDV were propagated in the laboratory. Thapsigargin, a SERCA inhibitor, was obtained from Sigma. Antibodies included SERCA2 ATPase antibody from Invitrogen and anti-PPRV/anti-NDV serum. The MTT assay was used to assess cytotoxicity. Antiviral efficacy was evaluated by plaque assay, measuring progeny virus production in the presence of Thapsigargin. The time-of-addition assay was performed by adding Thapsigargin at different time points post-infection. For SERCA2 overexpression, cells were transfected with pCR3-SERCA2 plasmid using Lipofectamine 3000. Immunofluorescence assays used primary and secondary antibodies to visualize viral protein localization. Thapsigargin-resistant mutants were selected by serial passaging of viruses in the presence of Thapsigargin. The study did not involve complex architectures or models; it focused on experimental manipulations and biological assays. The novelty lies in the use of a SERCA inhibitor to investigate its impact on paramyxovirus replication and the subsequent analysis of potential drug resistance.

Key Results
Thapsigargin at 0.5 µM did not significantly affect cell viability (Figure 1a, 1b). Thapsigargin significantly inhibited NDV (Figure 1c), PPRV (Figure 1d), and BHV-1 (Figure 1e) replication but not BPXV (Figure 1f). Thapsigargin did not exhibit virucidal effects (Figure 1g). Overexpression of SERCA2 rescued the inhibitory effect of Thapsigargin on NDV/PPRV replication (Figure 2a, 2b). PPRV infection induced SERCA2 expression in Vero cells (Figure 3a). Virus-induced SERCA2 expression could be blocked by Thapsigargin (Figure 3b). The time-of-addition assay showed that Thapsigargin inhibited NDV and PPRV replication most effectively when added before infection (Figure 4a, 4b). Thapsigargin inhibited paramyxovirus entry (Figure 5a, 5b). Thapsigargin treatment resulted in defective localization of viral proteins in approximately 70% of cells (Figure 6c, 6d). NDV, but not PPRV, developed resistance to Thapsigargin after 70 passages (Figure 7b, 7c). The P70-Thapsigargin virus showed significantly lower sensitivity to Thapsigargin compared to P0 and P70-Control viruses (Figure 7b).

Interpretation Implications
The findings suggest that SERCA is a crucial host factor for optimal paramyxovirus replication, specifically impacting viral entry and protein localization. The study validates SERCA as a potential target for antiviral drug development. The observation of NDV resistance to Thapsigargin, a host-targeting agent, adds to the understanding of viral adaptation mechanisms. This result implies that viruses can evolve to bypass their dependence on specific host factors, even when targeted by inhibitors. The study suggests that the mechanism of resistance might involve changes in host factor requirements or enhanced affinity for the natural substrate. The results support the idea that targeting host factors can be a promising approach for antiviral therapy, but resistance can still emerge.

Contradictions Limitations
The study acknowledges that the mechanism of NDV resistance to Thapsigargin is not fully understood. The study did not determine the specific viral proteins that acquired mutations conferring resistance. The study did not perform co-immunoprecipitation assays to confirm direct interactions between SERCA and viral proteins. The study's conclusions are limited by the lack of a complete understanding of the mechanism of resistance. The study did not report the random seed used for experiments. The study did not specify the exact experimental conditions for all assays, such as the exact MOI used during the selection of Thapsigargin-resistant viral mutants. The study did not include a detailed analysis of the mutations that arose in the NDV genome during the selection process. The study's conclusions are limited by the in vitro nature of the experiments, and the results may not fully translate to in vivo conditions.

Robustness Ablation Notes
The study performed a time-of-addition assay to determine the stages of the viral life cycle affected by Thapsigargin. The study also included a virucidal activity assay to rule out direct inactivation of virions.

Ethical Considerations
The study used cell lines and viruses available in the laboratory. No specific ethical considerations were mentioned, such as animal studies or human subjects.

Three Takeaways
(1) The core novel contribution is the identification of SERCA as a host factor that facilitates paramyxovirus replication and the demonstration that inhibiting SERCA with Thapsigargin reduces viral replication.

(2) The strongest evidence is that treatment with 0.5 µM Thapsigargin significantly inhibited NDV and PPRV replication (Figure 1c, 1d), and overexpression of SERCA2 rescued the inhibitory effect of Thapsigargin (Figure 2a, 2b).

(3) The primary limitation is the lack of a complete understanding of the mechanism of NDV resistance to Thapsigargin. Future research should focus on identifying the specific viral proteins that have acquired mutations conferring resistance and determining how these mutations affect the virus's interaction with SERCA.
