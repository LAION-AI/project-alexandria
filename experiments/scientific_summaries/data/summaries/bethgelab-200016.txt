Executive Summary
This study investigated conspecific negative density-dependence (CNDD) regulation on the survival and recruitment of Quercus costaricensis, a dominant oak species in the Talamanca highland forests of Costa Rica. The researchers hypothesized that, given the high density of seedlings, CNDD would be weak or absent. They tested this by analyzing the effects of conspecific seedling density, distance to the nearest adult tree, bamboo density, and herbivory on seedling survival and recruitment. The study found no significant effect of CNDD on seedling survival. However, bamboo density and herbivory significantly decreased seedling survival. Seedling height had the strongest positive effect on survival. Furthermore, seedling recruitment increased with conspecific seedling density. These results suggest that factors other than CNDD, such as herbivory and bamboo density, are more important in regulating seedling survival and recruitment. The lack of CNDD regulation may contribute to the dominance of Q. costaricensis in these highland forests. The study's novelty lies in demonstrating the absence of CNDD in a dominant oak species, which contrasts with the expected density-dependent regulation often observed in other tree species. This work is significant because it provides insights into the mechanisms that regulate population dynamics and forest composition in tropical highland forests. The main limitation is that the study did not include environmental factors such as soil conditions or temperature, which could influence CNDD regulation. The study also did not explore the mechanisms behind the observed herbivory and its impact on seedling survival.

Research Context
Conspecific negative density-dependence (CNDD) is a key mechanism proposed to regulate species coexistence in forests. Prior research has shown that CNDD often reduces seedling survival and recruitment in many tree species when conspecific density is high. However, the importance of CNDD may vary across species. Previous studies have shown that rare species are expected to be more affected by CNDD. In contrast, recent findings suggest that common species may be less affected due to physiological traits and genetic diversity. Tropical montane forests, unlike diverse lowland forests, are commonly dominated by a few tree species. This study addresses the gap in understanding the processes structuring these low-diversity forests by investigating the role of CNDD in the dominant oak species Q. costaricensis.

Research Question Hypothesis
The central research question was to determine if CNDD regulates seedling survival and recruitment in the dominant oak species Q. costaricensis in the highlands of Costa Rica. The primary hypothesis was that, given the high density of seedlings, CNDD regulation on seedling survival would be weak or absent. The study also explored the effect of distance to the nearest adult tree, bamboo density, and herbivory on seedling survival and recruitment. An alternative hypothesis was that seedling survival would be negatively affected by high conspecific seedling density, as predicted by the Janzen-Connell hypothesis. The study also hypothesized that the density of Chusquea bamboo would negatively affect seedling survival. The study aimed to test the Janzen-Connell hypothesis, which predicts that higher offspring mortality of abundant species reduces competition for other species, leading to increased species diversity. The study differed from prior work by focusing on a dominant oak species in a tropical montane forest, where the expectation was that CNDD would be weak or absent.

Methodological Details
The study was conducted at the Cerro de la Muerte Biological Station (CMBS) in Costa Rica. The study area is an upper montane tropical forest. Four 400-m2 plots (20 x 20 m) were established in old-growth forests. In February 2014, all Q. costaricensis seedlings (height < 2m) within each plot were mapped and tagged. The DBH (> 5 cm) of all adult trees within the plot and a 10 m wide buffer area were measured. For each seedling, height, distance to the nearest conspecific adult, and conspecific seedling density in a 1m radius were measured. Leaf damage was estimated using a qualitative index of herbivory (IH). The number of bamboo stems was counted for each quadrat. In May/June of 2015, 2016, and 2017, plots were re-censused to determine annual seedling mortality and recruitment per quadrat. A seedling was considered dead when its stem was dry, leafless, rotten, or missing in two consecutive censuses. The spatial distribution of seedlings was analyzed using the L(t)=(K(t)/π)0.5 transformation of the univariate Ripley's K function. Annual probability of seedling survival was modeled for three years (2014-2017) as a function of initial height, intraspecific seedling density, distance to nearest conspecific adult tree, herbivory, and Chusquea density using a generalized linear mixed-effects model with a binomial distribution. Quadrat and census year were included as random effects. A dummy variable (age) was included as a fixed factor. Seedling recruitment per quadrat was analyzed using a GLMM with a quasi-poisson distribution to estimate the effect of seedling density, conspecific tree density, and Chusquea density. All explanatory variables were standardized. Plots were included as a random effect. Statistical analyses were performed using the lme4 package in R (Version 3.2.2). The study did not specify any preregistration or multiple-comparison corrections.

Procedures Architectures
The study involved field observations and statistical modeling. The primary data collection involved mapping and tagging Q. costaricensis seedlings within four 20m x 20m plots. Seedling height, distance to the nearest adult tree, and conspecific seedling density were measured. Herbivory was assessed using a qualitative index. Bamboo stem density was also recorded. The study used generalized linear mixed-effects models (GLMMs) with a binomial distribution to analyze seedling survival. The models included fixed effects for initial height, intraspecific seedling density, distance to nearest conspecific adult tree, herbivory, and Chusquea density. Quadrat and census year were included as random effects. A dummy variable was included to identify seedlings from the 2014 census and those recruited in 2015. Seedling recruitment was analyzed using a GLMM with a quasi-poisson distribution. The models included seedling density, conspecific tree density, and Chusquea density as explanatory variables. Plots were included as a random effect. The study used the R statistical language (Version 3.2.2) and the lme4 package for statistical analyses. The spatial distribution of seedlings was analyzed using the 'spatstat' library in R. The study did not involve any specific architectural or computational models.

Key Results
In the 2014 census, 2031 seedlings were mapped and tagged. Herbivory was common, with over 99% of seedlings showing leaf damage. Annual seedling survival decreased slightly over the study period, from 73.2% in 2015 to 64.38% (2016) and 63.8% (2017). Seedling height had the strongest positive effect on seedling survival. Bamboo density and herbivory reduced survival. The effect of near adults on seedling survival was best described by a negative polynomial function. There was a significant interaction between seedling and Chusquea densities: high seedling density significantly increased seedling survival at low bamboo densities, and decreased survival at higher bamboo densities. In 2015, 1578 new recruits were recorded. Herbivory was lower in new recruits (22.9% leaf damage) compared to seedlings from the 2014 census (43.75% leaf damage; t = 25.798, p< 0.0001). Seedling recruitment significantly increased with conspecific seedling density. The study found no significant effect of CNDD on seedling survival. The study found that herbivory decreased at higher seedling densities and increased away from adults.

Interpretation Implications
The findings suggest that CNDD does not significantly regulate seedling survival or recruitment in Q. costaricensis. This contrasts with the expectation of the Janzen-Connell hypothesis. The lack of CNDD regulation may contribute to the dominance of this oak species in the montane forests. The study suggests that herbivory and bamboo density are more important factors in regulating seedling survival. The results support the hypothesis of herbivore satiation, where high seedling density may reduce the impact of herbivory. The study implies that the high density of Q. costaricensis seedlings may be due to predator satiation and adult facilitation through ectomycorrhizal transference. The findings also suggest that interspecific interactions, such as competition with bamboo, play a crucial role in regulating oak population dynamics.

Contradictions Limitations
The study found no evidence of CNDD regulation, which contradicts the expectations of the Janzen-Connell hypothesis. The study's main limitation is that it did not include environmental factors such as soil conditions or temperature, which could influence CNDD regulation and could affect the strength of CNDD. The study also did not explore the mechanisms behind the observed herbivory and its impact on seedling survival. The study focused on a single species and a specific geographic location, which limits the generalizability of the findings. The study did not report the random seed used for statistical analyses. The study did not investigate the role of ectomycorrhizal associations, which could provide an additional advantage to seedling establishment.

Three Takeaways
The primary novel contribution of this study is the demonstration that conspecific negative density-dependence (CNDD) does not significantly regulate seedling survival in the dominant oak species Q. costaricensis in a neotropical highland forest. The strongest evidence supporting this claim is the lack of a significant effect of conspecific seedling density on seedling survival in the GLMM models. The primary limitation is the lack of investigation into other environmental factors, such as soil conditions and temperature, that could potentially influence CNDD regulation. Future research should explore the influence of these factors, as well as the mechanisms behind the observed herbivory and its impact on seedling survival.
