Executive Summary
This paper investigates the effects of fluid inertia on the sedimentation dynamics of non-Brownian spheres, extending the study from the Stokes regime to moderately inertial regimes. The primary motivation is the limited understanding of sedimentation dynamics beyond the Stokes regime, despite its importance in various chemical industries. The study employed particle image velocimetry (PIV) to measure the sedimentation dynamics of a semi-dilute suspension of glass beads in glycerol-water mixtures, varying the Reynolds number (Re) from 0.001 to 2.3. The key finding is that inertial corrections to Stokes sedimentation begin when the inertial screening length, l = a/Re, becomes comparable to the Stokes sedimentation length, ξ0, at a critical Reynolds number, Rec ≈ 0.05. For Re > Rec, inertial screening significantly reduces both the magnitude and spatial extent of particle velocity fluctuations. The study also demonstrates that a modified Hinch force balance model, incorporating Reynolds number-dependent drag coefficients, accurately connects the fluctuation magnitudes to the correlation sizes across the entire range of Reynolds numbers studied. The novelty lies in experimentally demonstrating and quantifying the effects of inertial screening on sedimentation dynamics and connecting these observations to a modified Hinch model. This work is significant because it provides insights into the transition from Stokes to inertial sedimentation, which is crucial for understanding and modeling sedimentation processes in various applications. A limitation is the focus on a single particle size and volume fraction. The study also does not explore the effects of different particle shapes or fluid properties.

Research Context
The sedimentation dynamics of non-Brownian spheres is a fundamental problem, primarily studied in the Stokes regime (Re << 1). In this regime, particles exhibit large-amplitude velocity fluctuations with a characteristic spatial size, ξ, despite the infinite range of hydrodynamic interactions. Prior work has focused on understanding the origin of screening in Stokes sedimentation. However, the sedimentation dynamics at moderate Reynolds numbers (Re ~ 1) have received less attention. Existing studies lack experimental data describing the sedimentation dynamics beyond the Stokes regime. Cowan et al. (2000) examined fluctuation dynamics at moderate Re < 1 but concluded that velocity fluctuations were independent of Reynolds number. This paper addresses the gap in understanding by investigating the effects of inertia on sedimentation dynamics and providing experimental evidence to support the existence of inertial screening.

Research Question Hypothesis
The central research question is: How does fluid inertia affect the sedimentation dynamics of non-Brownian spheres? The primary hypothesis is that inertial corrections to Stokes sedimentation will occur when the inertial screening length, l = a/Re, becomes comparable to the Stokes sedimentation length, ξ0. This leads to the prediction that at a critical Reynolds number, Rec ≈ a/ξ0, inertial screening will reduce the magnitude and spatial extent of particle velocity fluctuations. An alternative hypothesis is that velocity fluctuations remain independent of Reynolds number for Re < 1, as suggested by Cowan et al. (2000). The study aims to determine the Reynolds number at which inertial effects become significant, quantify the changes in velocity fluctuations and correlation lengths, and assess the applicability of a modified Hinch model in describing the observed behavior. The study differs from prior work by experimentally investigating the transition from Stokes to inertial sedimentation and providing quantitative data on the effects of inertia on velocity fluctuations and correlation lengths.

Methodological Details
The study employed particle image velocimetry (PIV) to measure the sedimentation dynamics of a semi-dilute suspension of non-Brownian spheres. The experimental design aimed to investigate the sedimentation dynamics across a range of Reynolds numbers, extending from the Stokes regime to the moderately inertial regime. The sample consisted of monodisperse glass beads with a radius (a) of 137 ± 9 µm. These beads were dispersed in various mixtures of glycerol and water to achieve Reynolds numbers ranging from 0.001 to 2.3. The volume fraction (φ) of the particles was maintained at 0.06 in all experiments. The sample cell was a rectangular glass tube with dimensions of 8 × 80 × 305 mm. The temperature was maintained at the ambient value of 23 ± 1°C. Particle velocities were measured using a PIV apparatus from Dantec Instruments, which included specialized image processing software and hardware. The PIV system captured images of a large cross-section of the cell (3 × 3 cm), allowing for the simultaneous study of several thousand particles. Initial random dispersions were prepared by vigorous shaking of the cells. The location of the imaging window was far from both the sedimentation front and the sediment growth. Each velocity field was a map of 41 × 42 vectors, with each vector representing the local average of two to four spheres. The primary metric was the Reynolds number, defined as Re = 2Va/ν, where V is the particle velocity, a is the particle radius, and ν is the fluid kinematic viscosity. The normalized autocorrelation function of the z component of the velocity fluctuations, Cz(r) = ⟨δVz(0)δVz(r)⟩/⟨δVz(0)2⟩, was used to analyze the spatial correlations of the velocity fluctuations. The empirical forms Cz(z) = exp[−(z/ξ)1.5] and Cz(x) = exp[−(x/ξ⊥)1.5] were fit to the data. The ensemble-averaged root-mean-square (rms) velocity fluctuations, σv = sqrt(⟨[Vi,z − V]2⟩), were also calculated. No mention of preregistration or multiple-comparison corrections was made.

Procedures Architectures
The experimental procedure involved dispersing monodisperse glass beads in glycerol-water mixtures within a rectangular glass tube. The key to controlling the Reynolds number was the use of different glycerol-water mixtures, which altered the fluid's kinematic viscosity (ν). The PIV system, consisting of specialized image processing software and hardware, captured the particle velocities. The PIV system generated velocity maps by analyzing the displacement of particles between successive images. The velocity maps were then used to calculate the velocity fluctuations (δVi = Vi − ⟨Vi⟩) and their spatial correlations. The autocorrelation function, Cz(r), was calculated to quantify the spatial extent of the velocity fluctuations. The correlation lengths, ξ and ξ⊥, were determined by fitting the experimental data to empirical forms. The root-mean-square (rms) velocity fluctuations, σv, were calculated to quantify the magnitude of the velocity fluctuations. The Hinch model was modified to account for the effects of inertia. The modified Hinch model used the correlation length, ξ, and the Reynolds number-dependent drag coefficient, βRe. The model was then used to predict the normalized velocity fluctuations, σV/V, and compared with the experimental data. The software used for image processing and analysis was from Dantec Instruments. The hardware included the PIV apparatus. The study's novel aspect is the experimental investigation of inertial screening and the application of a modified Hinch model to explain the observed behavior. The study did not involve the construction of new architectures or systems.

Key Results
The study found that the onset of inertial corrections to Stokes sedimentation occurs at a critical Reynolds number of approximately 0.05. Figure 1 illustrates the changes in particle velocity fields with increasing Reynolds number. At Re = 0.001, the Stokes regime, large velocity fluctuations are observed. At Re = 0.2, the fluctuations appear slightly reduced. At Re = 2.3, the fluctuations are largely absent. Figure 2 shows the spatial correlation functions of the velocity fluctuations for Re = 0.001 and Re = 2.3. The decay lengths are much reduced in the inertial sample (Re = 2.3). Figure 3(a) shows the spatial correlation lengths, ξ and ξ⊥, as a function of Reynolds number. For Re < 0.05, ξ is independent of Re, and for Re > 0.05, ξ decreases with increasing Re. The decrease in ξ demonstrates that inertial screening occurs at shorter distances for higher Reynolds numbers. Figure 3(b) shows the normalized velocity fluctuations, σV/V, versus Re. σV/V is independent of Re for Re < 0.05, with a value of approximately 0.75. At Re = 2.3, σV/V is reduced by approximately 80%. The modified Hinch model, using the fit expression for ξ, accurately predicts σV/V across the entire range of Reynolds numbers studied. The model predictions show that the drop in fluctuation magnitudes with Re is due to both decreasing correlation lengths and the increasing drag force term. The study also re-examined the experiments of Cowan et al. (2000) and found that their results were consistent with the current findings.

Interpretation Implications
The findings support the hypothesis that inertial screening significantly affects sedimentation dynamics. The observed reduction in velocity fluctuations and correlation lengths with increasing Reynolds number indicates that inertia plays a crucial role in the transition from Stokes to inertial sedimentation. The success of the modified Hinch model in accurately predicting the normalized velocity fluctuations suggests that the underlying assumption of a random particle density distribution remains valid even in the presence of inertia. The study implies that the inertial screening length, l, and the Stokes correlation length, ξ0, are key parameters in determining the onset of inertial effects. The results provide an important benchmark for future theoretical work on moderately inertial systems. The study suggests that the Brenner criterion, which compares particle diffusion and viscous momentum diffusion, also accurately predicts the transition to inertial behavior. The findings broaden the understanding of sedimentation processes and provide a framework for modeling sedimentation in various applications.

Contradictions Limitations
The study's primary limitation is the focus on a single particle size and volume fraction, which limits the generalizability of the findings to other particle sizes and concentrations. The study does not explore the effects of different particle shapes or fluid properties. The study does not address the mechanisms underlying the inertial screening process in detail. The study's findings are consistent with the results of Cowan et al. (2000), who found no inertial effects for Re < 1, which is consistent with the current study's findings. The study does not explicitly address the potential for systematic errors in the PIV measurements. The absence of information on the random seed used for the experiments limits the reproducibility of the study. The study does not discuss any potential conflicts with prior literature, other than the comparison with Cowan et al. (2000).
